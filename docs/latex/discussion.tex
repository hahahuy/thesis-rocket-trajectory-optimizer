\chapter{DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS}


\section{Overall Performance of the Neural Surrogate}

The results indicate that the proposed neural surrogate is able to recover the 
overall structure of rocket trajectories when tested on previously unseen cases. In particular, 
the model reproduces the main trends in vertical position, velocity, and mass evolution, even 
though explicit control inputs are not provided during the inference stage. This outcome suggests 
that physics-based regularization terms, derived from the governing equations, introduce a useful 
inductive bias that supports trajectory reconstruction in complex, high-dimensional dynamical systems. 
It should be noted, however, that the proposed approach focuses on approximating optimal ascent 
trajectories, rather than learning a general closed-form state transition model. This limitation 
arises because the proposed approach approximates optimal ascent trajectories directly, rather 
than learning a general closed-form state transition model.

The strong agreement observed in the vertical position throughout most of the ascent phase further 
indicates that the neural network effectively captures the accumulated effects of thrust and acceleration 
over time. The behavior of a PINN-inspired model reflects typical observations in the literature concerning 
physics-informed training, in that reconstructed state variables follow smooth integral-structured dynamics 
and are usually reconstructed with greater accuracy than predicted values requiring a direct time derivative.

However, substantial errors developing at the end of the trajectory indicate that error accumulation still 
presents a challenge for long-term predictions. This observation has previously been demonstrated through 
research conducted using PINNs and their variants applied to nonlinear ordinary differential equations, in 
which it was found that the magnitude of the prediction error increased as the distance in time from the initial 
condition increased. Consequently, although the general shape of the trajectory was maintained, local inaccuracies 
became more pronounced at later stages in the motion.

\section{Interpretation of Velocity and Higher-Order Dynamics}

The vertical velocity results exhibit noticeably larger discrepancies than those observed for 
position, particularly during the later stages of the flight. This behavior is not unexpected, since 
velocity is obtained directly from first-order time derivatives of the network output. Since we take 
model-inspired by PINN, such derivative-based quantities are generally more sensitive to approximation 
errors and to stiffness effects that arise during training, which makes accurate reconstruction more 
difficult.

The growing divergence in predicted velocity near the terminal phase further suggests that the network 
tends to emphasize overall trajectory coherence rather than strict local accuracy of derivatives. In 
other words, the learning process appears to favor consistency at the global level, even if this comes 
at the expense of precise matching of velocity profiles at specific time points. This type of trade-off 
has been widely reported in studies of PINNs or its variants, where the minimization of combined data 
and physics losses does not necessarily lead to uniform accuracy across all state variables and their 
derivatives.

From a physical standpoint, velocity evolution is governed by forces that can change rapidly over short 
time intervals, such as thrust termination and variations in mass. Accurately resolving these effects 
typically requires either finer temporal sampling or a stronger and more targeted enforcement of the 
governing equations during such transition phases. While the current neural surrogate demonstrates 
acceptable overall behavior, these results indicate that velocity prediction remains a limiting factor 
when high-fidelity trajectory reconstruction is required.

\section{Mass Evolution and Constraints Enforcement}

The mass evolution results show that the network learns the general trend of propellant 
consumption during the burn phase and the constant-mass regime after cutoff. However, 
small oscillations around the terminal mass and multiple monotonicity violations are observed 
in the predicted trajectory.

These violations indicate that while the mass conservation law is included as a physics 
residual, it is not enforced as a hard constraint. As a result, the neural network allows small 
physically inconsistent fluctuations if they reduce the overall loss. This behavior highlights 
an important limitation of soft constraint enforcement in PINNs or  in this case, physics-regularized 
neural surrogates. This behavior highlights a fundamental limitation of penalty-based physics 
enforcement and underscores the distinction between physics-informed learning and 
physics-guaranteed modeling.

In practical aerospace applications, mass monotonicity is a non-negotiable physical constraint. 
The current results suggest that additional structural constraints or post-processing steps 
are necessary if the model is to be deployed in safety-critical settings. Nonetheless, the 
ability of the neural network to approximate the mass profile without explicit integration 
demonstrates the flexibility of the physics-informed approach.

\section{Horizontal Drift and Symmetry Breaking}

The horizontal position results show small yet persistent deviations from the reference trajectory, 
which is identically zero throughout the motion. This behavior demonstrates that the neural network 
does not necessarily maintain symmetry along dimensions of the problem not specifically restricted, 
even though the physical solution corresponding to zero displacement is expected to preserve this symmetry.

In neural network-based models of differential equations, drift is a common occurrence, particularly 
with respect to governing equations with trivial and/or stationary solutions. Without implementing 
specific penalties or restrictions to ensure symmetry within an optimization process, the resulting 
convergence may satisfy the overall loss function while remaining inconsistent with the defined symmetry 
of the physical problem at hand. In this manner, the trained model meets the training goal, but does not 
strictly adhere to the physics-associated symmetry.

This finding implies that it may be beneficial to include regularization terms or coordinate-specific 
constraints when modeling physical problems that require zero-motion states due to physics. Alternatively, 
if identified, the model formulation could be simplified by eliminating extraneous variables (i.e., degrees of 
freedom) resulting in less spurious predictions and improved robustness. This synergistic behavior would be 
enhanced by the lack of explicit control inputs applied to the residual form of the problem, as it means that 
enforcement of lateral force balance is not applied under current conditions.

\section{Quaternion Normalization and Attitude Representation}
The quaternion norm deviation results indicate that throughout the entire predicted trajectory the predicted 
orientation remains close to a unit norm, which suggests that the normalization techniques used during training 
were properly implemented and allowed for the effective maintenance of accurate and consistent attitude estimates 
in the prediction process.

This is an important feature of rotational dynamics, as even small departures from a unit norm can accumulate and 
produce attitudes that do not correspond to the physical motion of a body. Thus, the results obtained indicate that 
the formulation developed limits the occurrence of such deviations, while at the same time being stable enough to 
propagate without introducing numerical instability.

These results also support the findings in the literature regarding learning-based attitude models, where explicit 
normalization schemes or penalties are needed to maintain physically plausible orientations. The present results 
provide further evidence for the effectiveness of the approach chosen in this literature.

\section{Physics Residual Behavior and Model Consistency}

The examination of the residuals from the physics model provides a better understanding of how accurately the 
predicted solution matches with what would normally be predicted by the governing equations of motion in the 
standard way that a flight would behave. In general, the residual magnitudes stay within reasonable bounds over 
the duration of the simulation, and as time moves forward in the simulation, the availability of the force values 
decreases to the minimum values at the final time step. This pattern suggests that as time passes, the model is becoming 
progressively more consistent with the governing equations of motion.

However, the residuals do not have the same profile throughout the trajectory. The variability in the residuals from the 
beginning of flight through the end of flight demonstrates that there is a changing level of satisfaction of the constraints 
imposed by the equations of motion between different flight phases. This level of variability in the satisfaction of the 
constraints imposed by the equations of motion is further accentuated by the absence of explicit control inputs in the 
formulation of residuals. Therefore, since the lateral force balance from the control inputs is not explicitly imposed, it 
becomes possible for certain components of the residual to dominate during different phases of motion.

In the literature regarding PINNs and other approaches that utilize learning for system identification in nonlinear systems 
with time-varying dynamics, there has been evidence of this behavior. Each operating condition will have a different residual 
term that becomes prominent for that condition due to a shift in the balance of dominant physical phenomena. This illustrates 
the relevance of techniques such as adaptive loss weighting, or curriculum training, which are often designed to help 
alleviate the problem of consistently enforcing constraints when using these methods for modeling systems across multiple 
operational ranges.

\section{Practical Implications and Applicability}

On the basis of how the results obtained show, physics-regularized neural surrogate will provide a promising avenue 
for surrogate modelling for rocket trajectory reconstruction in an analysis context. The ability to provide complete 
state trajectory information without requiring discrete numerical integration points will benefit many applications 
of the approach, including rapid simulation tasks, early-stage design space exploration, and aircraft embedded onboard 
prediction systems where fast evaluations are required. Thus, the approach has the potential to be used in a wide range 
of applications, including but not limited to, trajectory optimization, trajectory tracking, and trajectory prediction.

However, the existence of constraint violations and significant inaccuracies in the derivative estimates indicate that 
the current formulation is primarily intended for analysis and approximation purposes, and not for direct use in closed-loop 
control systems. Thus, in real-world applications (including industrial deployment and mission-critical operations), it would 
be prudent to implement additional layers of verification, validation, and safety mechanisms prior to practical implementation 
(e.g. field testing or laboratory testing).

Nonetheless, the proposed approach is compelling in scenarios whereby computational efficiency and differentiability are 
of utmost importance. Optimization problems, inverse modelling, and sensitivity analyses are cases where the desired 
properties of the proposed approach are advantageous, albeit not all stages need to conform to strict physical fidelity. 
Thus, the approach is not limited to the analysis and approximation purposes, and can be used in a wide range of 
applications, including but not limited to, trajectory optimization, trajectory tracking, and trajectory prediction.

\section{Limitations of the Present Study}

Several limitations of the present study should be clearly recognized. First, the model is assessed 
using simulated data that are generated from the same physical assumptions employed during training. 
As a result, the ability of the approach to generalize to real flight data, where disturbances, 
uncertainties, and modeling errors are inevitably present, has not yet been examined.

Second, all physical constraints are enforced through soft penalty terms in the loss function. While 
this strategy offers flexibility during optimization, it also permits solutions that are not fully 
physically consistent when the training process prioritizes overall loss reduction. This effect can 
be observed most clearly in behaviors such as violations of mass monotonicity and the appearance of 
horizontal drift, as discussed in the previous sections.

Third, using a fixed timeframe and equal time intervals may also limit the ability to model different 
kinds of flights or abrupt changes in dynamics. These design choices make it easier to train and evaluate 
a model, but may limit the model's ability to adapt to more complicated situations.

The above limitations do not invalidate the outcomes obtained from this research. However, it does indicate 
that there are many parts of this framework that need to be improved upon in future work.

\section{Recommendations for Future Improvements}
Hybrid constraint enforcements can be created using physics-based loss terms and having hard constraints or 
projection-based correction methods. Specifically, properties such as mass monotonicity and symmetry being 
enforced directly in the model would likely increase the model's quality of physical consistency. This approach 
is preferable over relying on penalties throughout training.

Furthermore, adaptive loss weighting schemes and phase-aware training strategies have the potential to increase model 
performance in critical dynamic transitions, such as engine cutoff, where the governing dynamics are suddenly altered. 
To allow for greater flexibility and practicality of this framework, the incorporation of variable time horizons and 
stochastic disturbances into this framework must also be included.

Finally, the use of real-world flight data or higher-fidelity aerodynamic ratios will also enable a much more rigorous test of 
robustness and generalization of the hybrid constraint enforcement as described above. Including these data will permit 
evaluation of the proposed hybrid constraint enforcement in more realistic situations, therefore providing developed 
evidence for its applicability outside of controlled testing settings.
