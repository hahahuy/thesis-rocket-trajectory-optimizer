\chapter{Conclusion and recommendations}
\section{Conclusions}
This thesis examined whether a structured, physics-regularized neural surrogate is capable of 
serving as an effective surrogate model for reconstructing rocket ascent trajectories, and whether 
such a model can be integrated into a broader trajectory optimization framework 
if extended to explicitly condition on control inputs and enforce full dynamics. The study was 
motivated by the computational cost and limited flexibility of classical optimal control solvers 
when repeated trajectory evaluations or gradient information are required.

The results support the conclusion that physics-regularized neural surrogates 
constitute a promising surrogate modeling approach for rocket ascent trajectory reconstruction 
when the governing equations are well understood and training 
data are available. By incorporating physics-based regularization terms derived from the equations of motion into 
the learning objective, the proposed model is able to reconstruct 
full-state trajectories that are globally consistent with the dominant trends 
of the reference dynamics, without relying on explicit numerical integration during 
inference. This suggests that physics guidance can 
significantly improve the reliability of neural surrogates compared to 
purely data-driven models. All conclusions are restricted to simulated trajectories 
generated from the same modeling assumptions used during training.

In line with prior work on physics-informed learning, the study shows that 
state variables governed primarily by integral effects are captured more 
robustly than derivative-dominated quantities. This observation is 
consistent with findings reported by Raissi et al. and Karniadakis et al., 
where PINNs were shown to approximate smooth solution manifolds 
effectively while remaining sensitive to higher-order dynamics. The 
present work extends these insights to a six-degree-of-freedom rocket 
ascent problem with coupled translational, rotational, and mass dynamics.

At the same time, the thesis shows that relying solely on soft enforcement of 
physical constraints is not sufficient to ensure strict adherence to 
non-negotiable physical laws, such as mass monotonicity and symmetry preservation. 
Although the model is able to learn the overall structure of propellant consumption 
and the main characteristics of orientation dynamics, local violations can still 
occur when constraint satisfaction competes with data fidelity during training. 
This observation is consistent with recent theoretical analyses of physics-regularized 
neural surrogates optimization behavior, which highlight the inherent trade-offs 
between different loss components and the lack of hard guarantees when constraints 
are imposed only through penalty-based formulations.

The analysis shows that physics-constrained neural network substitutes should be utilized 
preferably for analysis/optimization rather than used directly as replacements for high-fidelity 
simulation in high-risk (safety critical) applications. The primary advantage of this new class 
of surrogate models is their ability to provide computationally efficient approximations that 
preserve many of the characteristics of actual complex dynamical systems; however, local-level 
verification of exact compliance with physics principles cannot always be guaranteed (e.g. linear 
simulation errors can accumulate with increased complexity.)

\section{Implications for Trajectory Optimization and Scientific Machine Learning}

The proposed surrogate approach highlights a potential path to decreasing reliance on repeated numeric 
integrations within the gradient-based optimization framework from a trajectory optimization standpoint. 
The model is entirely differentiable, hence allowing for direct computation of the initial condition sensitivities 
via formal derivative methods. Moreover, once control inputs are integrated into the model, we may formally 
compute the control parameter sensitivities. Generally, deriving these types of sensitivity analyses is 
challenging when using standard numerical solvers because sensitivity analyses require many evaluations of the problem.

In a broader context, within scientific machine learning, this research supports the viewpoint that the structural 
architecture of a neural network and a neural network’s loss function formulation may be as significant as the overall 
neural network capacity or ability. The examples presented in this research suggest that performance is very dependent on 
the manner in which the training objectives of the model and the predictions to be made by the model are aligned. The 
findings presented in this study provide practical support to ongoing efforts to develop PINNs and physics-constrained 
neural network models regarding issues related to robustness, constraint enforcement, and long-term prediction capabilities.

\section{Recommendations for Future Work}

Many recommendations for future work have been derived from the results of this research.

To begin with, when creating future models, it is recommended that future work look to include 
hard or semi-hard enforcement mechanisms alongside purely penalty-based loss functions placing 
constraints on critical physical laws such as mass monotonicity and symmetry conditions. Several 
approaches, including projection or constrained output parameterization methods and hybrid solver 
networks are recommended to employ the hard or semi-hard enforcement mechanisms. These types of 
approaches may provide a much more reliable and accurate expectation for the enforcement of the 
critical physical constraints than soft enforcement methods alone, particularly in those situations 
where it is unacceptable to accept the possibility of a violation of the critical physical constraints.

The second recommendation is to utilize either phase-aware or adaptive training strategies. This would 
allow rapid dynamic change periods (such as engine shutoff and thrust transition periods) to be handled 
more effectively. Dynamic adjustment to the loss weight during training or introducing a curriculum-based 
method of training could improve the accuracy of the derivative estimates while still maintaining consistency 
in trajectory profile over the entire time horizon.

Third, extending the framework to accommodate varying time horizons as well as non-nominal flight conditions 
(such as wind disturbance and parameter uncertainty) would allow for much greater relevance of the framework 
to real-world applications. In addition to increasing the practical relevance of the framework, it would also 
allow for much greater evaluation of how well the developed model generalizes to conditions that were not represented 
during training.

Finally, in the future work, it is highly recommended to combine experimental flight data and/or a more accurate 
simulation environment to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of this approach. To this end, if possible, 
combining physics-based neural networks with data assimilation techniques to construct hybrid models may provide 
an excellent opportunity to close the gap between simulation only models and models used for actual flight operations.

\section{Final Remarks}
This thesis demonstrates that by sufficiently designing and carefully evaluating Physics-Regularized Neural Surrogates, 
it can be meaningfully used as a model to reconstruct trajectory for rocket launch and ascent. While the PINN-inspired 
paradigms will not replace traditional simulation tools, they provide an alternative approach that can assist with quick 
model evaluations of desirable properties, allow for differentiation in some sense, 
and possess sufficient physical properties during learning of the trajectory.

The results from this study provide evidence supporting both strengths and limitations of Physics-Regularized Neural Surrogates 
as well as an avenue to continue to investigate the ability to use Machine Learning in a deeper way in trajectory analysis/optimization, 
and in addition to develop a broader Machine Learning Scientific Workflow.
